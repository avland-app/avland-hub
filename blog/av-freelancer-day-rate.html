Day rate or hourly? Pricing AV freelance work

November 4, 2026 · 6 min read

For most AV freelance work, a day rate with a stated number of hours is the better default, and an hourly rate is better for short or open-ended calls. A day rate gets you paid for the whole day, including load-in waiting and travel, and it is easy for a client to budget. Hourly pricing protects you when the call is short, unpredictable or likely to run long. Either way, the rate only works if you write down what it covers.

Below is how each model works, when to pick which, a side-by-side example, and how to state your rate so the invoice never surprises anyone.

How each rate works

A day rate is one price for a set block of hours, usually 8 or 10. A half-day rate covers a shorter block, often 4 to 5 hours. Hours past the block are billed as overtime (see how to calculate overtime for AV crew calls).

An hourly rate bills every hour worked, usually with a minimum call. The minimum matters: without one, a 2-hour call can cost you a whole day of availability.

To compare the two, convert the day rate: day rate ÷ hours it covers = effective hourly rate. A $600 day covering 10 hours is $60 an hour.

When a day rate is the better choice

  • Show days and installs with a fixed call. You are blocking the whole day. A day rate says so.
  • Long calls with slow stretches. Waiting for the LED wall to be ready is still your day.
  • Clients who budget by line. One number per person per day is easy to put on a quote and approve.
  • Travel days. If the day is gone either way, a flat rate (or a stated reduced travel-day rate) is simpler than counting hours.

When hourly is the better choice

  • Short calls: a 3-hour rehearsal, a tech check, a remote support call.
  • Open-ended work: troubleshooting, programming, patching a room where nobody knows how long it will take.
  • Work you can double-book: if you can take another gig around it, hourly with a minimum is fair to both sides.

How to set the number

Start from what you need a working day to pay, not from what the last client offered. Count the days you realistically book in a month, not every weekday: gaps between gigs, travel days and unpaid admin are part of the job. Divide your monthly target by booked days, then check the result against what AV companies near you pay for the same position.

Price different positions separately. An A1, a video engineer and a stagehand do not share a rate, and your rate should rise for specialist work like programming or show calling. Review it once a year, and raise it for new clients first so existing ones are not surprised.

What to put in writing

Whichever you pick, send this before the call, in the booking email or on your invoice terms:

  • The rate and how many hours it covers.
  • The minimum call or half-day rate.
  • When overtime and double time start, and at what multiple.
  • Travel and parking: billed, flat or included.
  • Cancellation terms, such as what you charge if a day is cancelled inside 48 hours.

It also helps to know how you are working. A freelancer billing as an independent contractor sets their own rate and terms by agreement. The U.S. Department of Labor notes that independent contractors are not covered by the FLSA, while employees are, and that status depends on the economic reality of the relationship rather than on the title or a signed agreement. This is general information, not legal advice; ask an accountant or employment attorney about your own setup.

Questions

Should an AV freelancer charge a day rate or an hourly rate?

Use a day rate for full show days and installs with a fixed call, and an hourly rate with a minimum for short or open-ended work. State what the rate covers either way.

How do you convert a day rate to an hourly rate?

Divide the day rate by the hours it covers. A $600 day covering 10 hours is $60 an hour.

What is a half-day rate in AV?

A half-day rate is a lower flat price for a short call, often 4 to 5 hours. Many freelancers use it as the minimum call so a short booking still pays fairly.

Do I need to write down my rate?

Yes. Put the rate, the hours it covers, the minimum call and your overtime start time in the booking email or invoice terms so everyone agrees before the call.
