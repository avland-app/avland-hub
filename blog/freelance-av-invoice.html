The freelance AV invoice: what to include

October 23, 2026 · 5 min read

A freelance AV invoice needs your name and contact details, the AV company you're billing, an invoice number and date, the gig details (event, venue, dates, position), one line per charge with hours or days and a rate, a total, and your payment terms. That's it. If the person in accounts payable can match it to the booking and pay it without emailing you, it's a good invoice.

Below is what to put on it, in what order, and the small details that decide whether you get paid in two weeks or chase it for two months.

What AV companies need to see before they pay

The production manager who booked you knows the gig. The person who pays you usually doesn't. Your invoice has to make sense to someone who wasn't at the show and is looking at it next to 30 others.

That person is checking three things:

  • Is this a real booking? The event name, show dates and the name of whoever booked you let them match it to the job.
  • Do the numbers match what was agreed? Your rate, hours and any extras should line up with the booking email or crew sheet.
  • Can I pay this? They need your legal name or business name, how you want to be paid, and, for most US companies, a W-9 on file.

Miss any of the three and the invoice goes into a pile marked "questions."

The parts of a freelance AV invoice

Use the same layout every time. Here's the order that works:

  1. Your details. Name or business name, phone, email, and your logo if you have one.
  2. Bill to. The AV company's name and the contact who booked you. If they have a separate accounts payable email, use it.
  3. Invoice number and date. Number every invoice in sequence (for example 2026-014). It makes your own records easier and it's the first thing anyone asks for on a follow-up call.
  4. Gig details. Event or client name, venue, show dates, your position (A1, V1, LD, stagehand), and the company's job or PO number if they gave you one.
  5. Line items. One line per charge: labor days or hours, overtime, travel, parking, kit or gear rental. Each line shows quantity, rate and amount.
  6. Total. Subtotal, any reimbursed expenses, and the amount due.
  7. Payment terms. When it's due, and how to pay you.

How to write the line items

Line items are where most back-and-forth starts. Write each one so it can be checked against the crew call without a phone call.

A few rules that save emails:

  • One line per day on multi-day gigs, with the date. If one day ran long, the overtime line points to that day.
  • Say what the day rate covers. "10 hr day" on the line tells the reader when overtime starts, so the OT line doesn't look like a surprise.
  • Keep expenses on their own lines. Parking, mileage, bag fees and per diem are often coded differently from labor on the company's side. Separate lines let them do that without asking you to reissue.
  • Attach receipts for anything you're being reimbursed for, if the company asks for them.

Getting paid on time: terms, W-9 and timing

Put your terms on every invoice, even if the company has its own. "Due within 30 days of invoice date" or "Net 15" is enough. Then list how to pay you: ACH, check, or whatever payment apps you accept. If you charge a late fee, say so on the invoice, not after the fact.

Most US companies that pay you as an independent contractor will ask for a Form W-9 before the first payment. Send it with your first invoice to a new company instead of waiting to be asked. A missing W-9 is one of the most common reasons a first payment sits. For anything beyond that, like how to handle the 1099 you get back, talk to your accountant.

Send the invoice soon after the gig, while everyone still remembers the show. An invoice sent the next day gets matched to the crew sheet quickly. One sent six weeks later gets questions.

Mistakes that slow down payment

  • No invoice number. Hard to track, hard to follow up on.
  • "Labor: $1,500" as a single line. The company can't check it against the call times, so they ask.
  • Billing the wrong entity. Some AV companies run shows under a different legal name than the one on the trucks. Ask once, then save it.
  • No job or PO number when the company uses them. Many accounts payable teams can't approve without it.
  • Payment details missing or buried. If they have to email you for your address or bank details, add a week.
  • Not tracking what's been paid. If you don't know which invoices are outstanding, you can't follow up on them.

Questions

What should a freelance AV invoice include?

Your name and contact details, the AV company and contact you're billing, an invoice number and date, the event, venue, show dates and your position, one line per charge with quantity and rate, the total, and your payment terms and payment method.

Should I bill overtime on a separate line?

Yes. Put overtime on its own line with the date and hours, and note on the day-rate line how many hours the day rate covers. That way the company can check it against the call times.

Do I need to send a W-9 with my invoice?

Most US companies ask independent contractors for a Form W-9 before paying them the first time. Sending it with your first invoice to a new company avoids a delay. Ask your accountant about your own tax situation.

When should I send the invoice?

As soon as the gig wraps, ideally within a day or two. Invoices sent while the show is fresh are easier for the company to match and approve.
