The payment terms that get AV freelancers paid fastest are short, written down before the gig, and tied to a date: "Net 15 from the invoice date" beats "paid when the client pays us." Put the terms in your booking message, repeat them on the invoice, and send the invoice within a day of the show. Most slow payments aren't about the money. They're about a vague deadline and a missing piece of paperwork.

Here's what to put in your terms, which ones to avoid, what California requires for freelancers, and how to follow up without souring the relationship.

The terms that actually speed things up

You don't need a legal document. You need five lines that answer the questions accounts payable will otherwise ask you later.

  • Due date. "Net 15" or "Net 30" counted from the invoice date, or a fixed date such as "due the 15th of the following month." Pick one and use it every time.
  • Invoice timing. "I invoice within 24 hours of the last day." The clock only starts when the invoice lands, so a late invoice is a late payment.
  • What the invoice includes. Dates, call and out times, rate, expenses with receipts, and any PO or job number the company needs.
  • How you get paid. ACH, check or the payment link you accept, and who the payee is.
  • Late fee or follow-up rule. Optional, but if you use one, state it before the job, not after.

Shorter terms get paid sooner. A company used to long terms from freelancers who never asked may agree to 15 or 30 when it's written into the booking confirmation. Whether they agree is a good thing to learn before you take the job.

Terms that slow you down

Some phrases look like terms but leave the date open:

VagueWhy it stallsSay this instead
Paid when the client pays usYour date depends on someone you don't work forNet 30 from invoice date
Paid on the next pay runPay runs may be monthly, and invoices that miss the cutoff waitAsk for the cutoff date, then invoice before it
Paid after the event wrapsNo end dateNet 15 from the last show day
No terms mentionedYou get the company's default, which may be much longer than you wantState yours in the booking message

"Pay-when-paid" wording is a common one in construction and install work, and it moves the client's payment risk onto you. If a company insists on it, ask what the longest realistic wait is and decide whether the rate covers that wait.

What California requires for freelancers

If you work in California or for California companies, a state law now covers some of this. California's Freelance Worker Protection Act, signed in 2024, applies to contracts entered into or renewed on or after January 1, 2025. In general terms, for jobs worth $250 or more (counting what the same company hired you for in the previous 120 days), the contract has to be in writing. It must list the services and rate, and say when you'll be paid or how that date is set. If the contract doesn't give a payment date, the company has to pay no later than 30 days after you finish the work. The full text of SB 988 has the exact definitions and exclusions.

That gives you a floor, not a ceiling. You can still ask for Net 15. The practical point is that a written booking confirmation with a pay date is now the normal thing to ask for, so asking doesn't make you difficult. Other states have their own rules, and how the law applies to your situation is a question for a lawyer or accountant. This post is general information, not legal advice.

Get the paperwork right so nothing sits in a queue

Many late payments are really stuck payments. The invoice is waiting on something small:

  1. Vendor setup. Send your W-9 and payment details when you accept the gig, not after the show.
  2. PO or job number. Ask for it up front and put it on the invoice.
  3. Right recipient. Ask who receives invoices, and email that address, not just the person who booked you.
  4. Receipts. Attach them to the invoice, as covered in the guide to what to include on a freelance AV invoice.

Following up without burning the relationship

Keep it routine and dated. A simple schedule works:

  • Day of due date: nothing. Give accounts payable the day.
  • 3 days past due: a short email. "Checking that invoice 1042 for the Nov 12 show was received and is scheduled. Anything you need from me?"
  • 10 days past due: a call to the person who books you, and a second email to accounts payable.
  • 30 days past due: a firm written notice, and stop accepting new gigs from that company until the invoice is paid.

Most of the time the answer is "we didn't have your W-9" or "it went to the wrong person," and the first email fixes it. Save the sharp tone for the rare company that keeps going quiet.

Questions

What payment terms should an AV freelancer use?

Net 15 or Net 30 from the invoice date, stated in your booking message and repeated on the invoice. Shorter is better, and Net 15 is a reasonable first ask.

How long does a company have to pay a freelancer in California?

Under California's Freelance Worker Protection Act, for covered contracts, the company pays on the date in the contract, or within 30 days of finishing the work if the contract names no date. Check the law's text and ask a professional how it applies to you.

Should I charge a late fee?

You can, but only if you stated it before the job. Many freelancers skip fees and rely on short terms and a clear follow-up schedule instead.

When should I send the invoice?

Within a day of your last show day. The payment clock usually starts when the invoice is received, so every day you wait is a day added to your wait.
