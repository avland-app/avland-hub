To price an AV installation, quote it room by room: equipment, materials, labor hours, programming and commissioning, then a contingency line, each priced on its own. A single lump sum for "the building" hides where the money goes and makes any change order an argument.

Here is how to scope one room, price each part, and roll the rooms up into a quote a facilities manager or GC can approve.

Scope the room before you price anything

The number that sinks installs is the one you guessed before the site visit. AVIXA's guide to AV needs assessments puts space analysis near the top of the process: room size and layout, acoustics, lighting, and power and infrastructure, ideally measured on a site visit. Do that first, then write down for each room:

  • Use: boardroom meetings, video calls, presentations, training, or a mix.
  • Seats and sightlines: how many people, and how far the back row is from the display.
  • Infrastructure: conduit paths, ceiling type, wall construction, power at the display and rack, network drops.
  • Who provides what: the client's IT, the electrician, the GC. Every "they will handle it" goes in the quote notes.

Break each room into five parts

Use the same five lines for every room so the quote is easy to read and easy to compare room to room.

PartWhat goes in it
EquipmentDisplays, mics, DSP, control, codec, mounts, rack
MaterialsCable, connectors, plates, conduit, hardware, consumables
LaborPre-wire, mounting, termination, rack build, install
Programming and commissioningDSP and control programming, tuning, testing, client sign-off
ContingencyA stated percentage for the unknowns you can't see yet

Price labor in hours, then check it against the room

Estimate each task in hours, multiply by the crew rate, and keep a separate line for programming. Programming is skilled work that does not scale with room size, so a small huddle room can need nearly the same programming as a larger one.

Then sanity-check against the site. Plan for slow ceilings, long cable runs, lifts, and working around a client's schedule. If the work happens after hours, say so and price it that way. For crew on your payroll, overtime follows labor law, so build that into the hours.

Add materials the way you buy them

Materials are the line items people forget because each one is small: connectors, wall plates, patch cables, mounting hardware, labels, fasteners. Add them as a defined line per room, based on your last similar job, rather than leaving them to absorb into labor. If you mark up equipment and materials, apply the same rule to every room.

State what's excluded and what triggers a change order

Installs go over when scope drifts. Put the boundaries on the quote:

  • What is not included: electrical work, drywall, painting, network switches, client-supplied devices.
  • The assumptions the price depends on: access, ceiling type, a working network, final room layout.
  • What happens when an assumption turns out wrong: a written change order before the work, not after.
  • Payment milestones, such as a deposit, equipment delivery, and completion.

Roll the rooms up and keep prices consistent

Once each room has its five parts, total the rooms and show a project subtotal. Keep the same line names and rates across rooms; clients notice when the same mic costs two different prices in two rooms.

A saved item library helps here. In GaffDesk you build your equipment and labor items once and reuse them on every quote, so a ceiling mic costs the same in room 1 and room 6. Whatever tool you use, the point is that your numbers come from one place.

Turn the approved quote into a plan

An approved install quote should flow straight into a material list and a schedule, so the person pulling parts is working from the same numbers you sold. FAQ

Should I charge for the site visit?

Many installers do, and credit it against the project if the client signs. Whatever you choose, state it in writing before you go.

How much contingency should I add?

There is no standard figure. Use a percentage you can defend from your own past jobs, and show it as its own line so the client sees it is deliberate.

Should I give one price per room or an itemized quote?

Give both: an itemized breakdown for the technical reader and a room total for whoever signs. The itemized version also protects you when scope changes.

Can I reuse a quote from a similar room?

Yes, as a starting point. Copy it, then check the room against your scope list so you do not carry over assumptions that no longer apply.
