There is no single going rate for a freelance AV technician. What you can charge depends on your position, your market and how much of the day a client is really buying. The reliable way to set it is to start from the wage benchmark for the job, add what freelancing costs you, and check the result against what AV companies near you actually pay.

This guide walks through that in four steps, with example numbers you can swap for your own.

Start with a benchmark, not a guess

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks pay for broadcast, sound and video technicians, the group that includes audio and video technicians. It reports a median of $28.92 an hour (about $60,150 a year) as of May 2025. The same page says evening, weekend and holiday work is common in the field and that technicians set up and take down equipment for live events.

Treat that number as a floor for thinking, not a rate to quote. It is a median across a whole occupation, it is a wage for an employee, and it covers every region and experience level. A specialist in a big market, such as a lead A1 or a video engineer, will usually sit well above it. A first-year stagehand may sit below.

Why a freelancer needs more than the wage

An employee's hourly wage is not the same as your cost of working for yourself. A freelancer pays for things an employer normally covers, and is paid only for days booked. Before you set a rate, account for:

  • Unbooked days. Few freelancers work every weekday. Count the days you realistically book in a month.
  • Self-employment taxes and insurance. Ask an accountant what applies to you. See our general guide to 1099 basics for AV freelancers.
  • Your own kit. Headphones, tools, cables, a laptop, transport.
  • Admin time. Quoting, invoicing and chasing payment are unpaid hours.

A simple way to see the effect: if an employee earns $29 an hour, a freelancer who books only about two thirds of the days has to charge noticeably more per booked hour just to land in the same place, before any of the costs above. That is why freelance rates look high next to wages and are not.

Build your rate in four steps

  1. Pick the income you want in a year. Use your own figure.
  2. Add what you pay for yourself. Taxes, insurance, kit, software, travel you do not bill.
  3. Divide by the days you realistically book. Not 260. Use your real number.
  4. Check the result against local AV companies. Ask peers what a position pays near you, and look at what companies list.

Price the position, not the person

An A1, a video engineer, a lighting tech and a stagehand do not share a rate. Specialist skills (programming, show calling, running a switcher) justify more, and so does being the person who can fix a problem alone. Keep one rate per position, and say which one you are booking.

Then state what the rate covers. Our guide to day rate or hourly covers how to word that, and the overtime guide covers what happens past the base hours.

Half days, travel and short calls

Your day rate is only the start. Decide in advance how you handle the cases that come up every season:

  • Half day. A short call still blocks part of your day, so many freelancers charge a half-day rate that is more than half the full rate.
  • Travel days. Either bill a reduced travel-day rate or a flat fee, and say which before you accept.
  • Late cancellations. If a client cancels the day before, you may have lost another booking. A short cancellation term in your booking message keeps that fair.
  • Overtime. Name the hour it starts and the multiple, so nobody is surprised on the invoice.

Put these in the same message as your rate. A client who has read the terms before the call rarely argues about them after it.

When to raise your rate

  • You are turning down work because you are booked.
  • You have added a skill clients pay extra for.
  • A year has passed since your last change.

Raise it for new clients first. Existing clients can move at their next booking, with notice in writing.

Questions

How much should a freelance AV technician charge per day?

It depends on position, market and experience, so there is no one number. Work it out from the income you need divided by the days you realistically book, then compare it to what local AV companies pay for the same role.

Is the BLS median wage what I should charge?

No. It is a median wage for employees across a whole occupation. A freelancer also covers taxes, insurance, kit and unbooked days, so the rate you need is usually higher.

Should I charge the same rate to every client?

Keep one rate per position, and change it deliberately, not per client mood. Some freelancers offer a lower rate for steady repeat work, but write it down.

How often should I raise my rates?

At least once a year, or sooner if you are regularly booked solid. Apply it to new clients first.
