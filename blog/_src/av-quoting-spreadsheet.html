A spreadsheet is a fine way to quote AV work while you do a few jobs a month and every one looks alike. It starts costing you when quotes get revised, when crew calls cross into overtime, and when the same price lives in ten copied tabs. The test is simple: if you ever wonder which file is the real one, you've outgrown it.

Why almost everyone starts in a spreadsheet

It's free, you already know it, and it bends to any job. You can build a rental quote in an afternoon: gear in rows, days in a column, a SUM at the bottom. For a shop doing a handful of quotes a month, that works, and there's no shame in it. Plenty of good AV companies ran this way for years.

The trouble isn't the first quote. It's the fortieth, built by copying the thirty-ninth.

Where spreadsheet quoting breaks down

Copy-paste becomes your database

Most spreadsheet quoting works by duplicating last job's file and editing it. Every copy carries its own prices. Raise the rate on a wireless mic in one file and the other thirty files still say the old number. Nothing tells you which quote used which rate, and nothing stops a stale price from going out.

One wrong formula, and nobody sees it

A spreadsheet gives you no warning when a SUM range stops one row short or a formula gets overwritten with a typed number. The total just looks like a total. Research on this is not new. A 2001 conference paper from the University of Greenwich, summarizing lab experiments by researcher Raymond Panko, reported that 51% of the 1,170 spreadsheets built in those experiments contained errors. Those were test spreadsheets built by students and professionals, not AV quotes, so don't read it as your error rate. The point is that errors in a hand-built spreadsheet are normal, not rare, and a quote is a spreadsheet where an error is money you either lose or have to explain to a client.

Overtime math lives in your head

A crew call that runs 14 hours needs regular, overtime and double-time hours at different rates. In a spreadsheet that means a hand-built formula per labor line, adjusted every time the rule changes or a day doesn't fit it. Mistakes here are expensive, because labor is usually where the margin is. Our guide to calculating overtime for AV crew calls shows the math you'd have to rebuild.

Revisions turn into "final_v3_REAL.xlsx"

Clients change their minds. Without a system, each change is either an overwrite, which erases what you originally quoted, or a new file with a name only you understand. When the client says "I thought we agreed on the other number," you want the exact earlier version in one click, not a search through your downloads folder.

The quote and the invoice are separate files

After the show, you retype the approved quote into an invoice. Every retyped line is a chance to drop an item, mistype a quantity, or forget the overtime you quoted. And if two people share the file, you're one accidental edit away from a changed price.

The PDF is always a chore

A spreadsheet's print layout is rarely a client-ready quote. Adding your logo, terms, page breaks and clean columns takes time on every job, and a quote that looks rough makes your price look softer.

What you actually need instead

You don't need fancy software. You need five things, whatever tool provides them:

  • One price list. Every item and labor rate stored once, so a change updates every future quote.
  • Reusable bundles. The parts that always go together, added in one click so nothing is left off.
  • Labor rules that calculate. Enter the hours; the regular, overtime and double-time split follows from your rules.
  • Saved revisions. Every version kept, with the approved one clearly marked.
  • Quote to invoice without retyping. The approved quote becomes the invoice.

You can get partway there in a spreadsheet with a locked price tab, lookup formulas and discipline. Be honest about whether you'll maintain that discipline at 10 quotes a month, when you're also running shows.

Questions

Is a spreadsheet good enough for AV quoting?

For a small number of similar jobs, yes. It stops being good enough when you revise quotes often, calculate crew overtime by hand, or retype quotes into invoices.

What are the biggest risks of quoting in Excel or Google Sheets?

Stale prices in copied files, formula errors that nobody notices, overtime calculated inconsistently, and lost earlier versions of a quote.

Can I fix a spreadsheet instead of switching?

Partly. Keep one locked price tab, pull prices with lookup formulas, and save every revision under a clear name. It takes discipline, and it still won't turn the approved quote into an invoice for you.
