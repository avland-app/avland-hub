To estimate labor hours for an AV install, break the job into tasks per room, give each task a base time from your own past jobs, then adjust for site conditions such as ceiling height, access and pulling cable through finished spaces. Add travel, programming, commissioning and a contingency, and keep the hours visible so you can compare them to what the job actually took.

Labor is where install quotes win or lose money. Gear has a price list. Hours are a guess until you have a method, so here is one that works room by room.

Start with tasks, not a total

A single number like "40 hours for the boardroom" can't be checked later. List the tasks instead, so that when the job runs over you can see which one was wrong. A typical room breaks down like this:

TaskWhat it covers
Site prep and receivingUnload, stage, check the pull against the material list
Rough-inBacking, boxes, conduit or pathway, cable pulls
MountingDisplays, speakers, cameras, racks and shelves
Termination and wiringConnectors, plates, patching, labels
Programming and DSP setupControl, audio tuning, network settings
Commissioning and trainingTest every input, walk the client through it, hand over
Travel and adminDrive time, parking, permits, paperwork

This lines up with the five parts of a room quote in how to price an AV installation by room. Labor is one of those five, so each task above is a line under it.

Use a base time, then adjust it

Industry labor guides exist, and they work the same way: a base time per item that you then adjust. The NSCA publishes a Labor Installation Standard built from a survey of its members, with per-item hours for audio, video, cabling, racks and more. It says plainly that "the labor units listed should be adjusted based on the conditions of each of your projects", and it lists reasons such as installer experience and training, non-productive time on site, and job-specific requirements like security clearance or safety training. It also warns that the guide is not a complete labor guide for everyone. It is a starting point, not a quote.

Your best base times are your own. After every job, write down actual hours per task. After five or six similar rooms you have averages you trust more than any table.

The conditions that change the hours

Ask these on the site walk, and write the answers next to the room:

  • Height. A display at 8 feet is a different job from a speaker at 16 feet that needs a lift. The NSCA guide separates normal and difficult work partly by mounting height.
  • Ceiling and wall type. Drop tile, hard lid, concrete, glass. Each changes mounting and cable routing.
  • Cable path. New construction with open walls is fast. A finished office where every run is fished takes far longer.
  • Access. Loading dock or four flights of stairs, elevator reservations, and work only after 6 p.m.
  • Other trades. If power, network or backing is someone else's job, your crew waits when they are late.
  • Crew experience. A senior tech and a new helper do not move at the same pace, and the plan should say who is on site.

Count people and hours separately

Write both numbers: the hours the task takes and how many people are on it. Two techs for six hours is twelve labor hours, not six. This is the most common slip when hours from a guide or an old job get copied into a new quote with a different crew size.

Add the parts people leave out

Three lines are easy to forget because they are not "installing":

  • Programming and commissioning. Give them their own hours, not a bit of whatever is left. A control system or DSP with several rooms can take longer than the physical install.
  • Travel and admin. Drive time and trips for a missing part cost hours even though nothing was mounted.
  • Contingency. Add a stated percentage for unknowns based on how well you know the site. A repeat client in a building you know needs less than a first visit to an old building.

FAQ

How do I estimate labor hours for an AV install?

Break each room into tasks, assign base times from your past jobs or an industry guide, adjust for site conditions, then add programming, commissioning, travel and a contingency.

Are there standard labor hours for AV installation?

Industry groups such as the NSCA publish labor unit surveys, but they are starting points that you adjust for each job. Your own records from past installs are usually more accurate.

How much contingency should I add?

There is no fixed number. Use a higher percentage for first visits, old buildings and unclear scope, and a lower one for repeat work on sites you know.

Should programming be in the install hours?

Quote it as its own line. Programming and commissioning are real labor, and hiding them inside general install time makes the estimate impossible to check afterward.
