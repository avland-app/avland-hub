To invoice an AV company as a freelance tech, settle the billing details before the gig, keep your own record of hours and expenses during it, and send the invoice within a day or two of strike to the right person, with a W-9 if it's your first job for them. Then follow up on the due date, not three weeks after it. Most slow payments come from a step that got skipped, not from a company that refuses to pay.

Here's that process, step by step, from the booking text to the money landing.

Before the gig: get the billing details once

The easiest time to fix a billing problem is before you've worked the show. When a new company books you, ask a few questions in the same thread as the booking:

  • Who do I bill? The legal company name, not the name on the truck. Some AV companies run shows through a different entity.
  • Where do invoices go? Many companies want invoices sent to an accounts payable address, not to the production manager who booked you.
  • Is there a job or PO number? If they use one, it has to be on the invoice or it can't be approved.
  • What does the day rate cover? How many hours, when overtime starts, and whether travel days or prep days are paid.
  • What are your payment terms? Net 15, Net 30, or something else. Better to hear it now than to find out on day 31.

Get the rate and the hours in writing, even if it's just a text that says "$650/10, OT after 10 at 1.5x." That message is what you'll point to if a line on your invoice gets questioned.

If you've never worked for this company, send your IRS Form W-9 now, or with your first invoice. US companies that pay you as an independent contractor need it on file to report what they paid you, and a missing W-9 is a common reason a first payment sits.

During the gig: keep your own record

Don't rely on the crew sheet alone. Write down your real call and out times for each day, the meal break, and anything that changed: an early call, a second room, a load-out that ran past midnight. Thirty seconds on your phone at the end of each day is enough.

Keep receipts for anything you'll bill back, like parking, tolls or a cable you bought on the way in. Take a photo of each one the same day. Paper receipts fade and wallets get cleaned out.

If the job grows while you're on site, for example you're asked to stay for a second day or run a breakout room nobody planned for, confirm the change with whoever booked you before you leave the venue. A quick "Confirming I'm on tomorrow 7a–5p, same rate" saves an argument later.

After strike: send it within a day or two

Invoice while the show is still fresh in everyone's mind. The production manager can match your hours to the crew sheet in a minute the day after. Six weeks later, they have to go digging, and your invoice waits.

When you send it:

  1. Send to the address they gave you, and copy the person who booked you so they know it went in.
  2. Use a clear subject line, like "Invoice 2026-018, Smith A1, Pier 3 Ballroom, Mar 12–13." Accounts payable teams search their inbox by these details.
  3. Attach a PDF, not a spreadsheet or a photo of a paper invoice. Name the file with the invoice number.
  4. Attach receipts for any expenses you're billing back, in the same email.
  5. Include the W-9 if this is your first invoice to the company.

Keep one invoice per gig unless the company asks you to bundle. Separate invoices are easier for them to approve one at a time, and easier for you to track.

When it's late: follow up on a schedule

Put the due date on your calendar the moment you send the invoice. If the money hasn't arrived by then, follow up that day. A short, friendly note works best:

  • On the due date: "Hi, checking on invoice 2026-018 for the Mar 12–13 show, due today. Let me know if you need anything from me." Attach the invoice again.
  • One week late: follow up again, and copy the person who booked you. They often know who's holding it up.
  • Two to three weeks late: call. Ask if there's a problem with the invoice and when you can expect payment.

Most late invoices aren't disputed. They're stuck: wrong inbox, missing PO number, a manager who hasn't approved it. Your follow-up is usually what gets them moving. If you charge a late fee, it has to be in your terms on the invoice from the start, not added once it's overdue.

Questions

Who should I send my invoice to at an AV company?

Send it to the billing or accounts payable address the company gives you, and copy the person who booked you. If no one has told you, ask when you're booked.

How soon after a gig should I invoice?

Within a day or two of strike. Invoices sent while the show is fresh are matched to the crew sheet quickly and approved sooner.

Do I need to send a W-9 to every AV company?

Send one to each new company you work for as an independent contractor, before or with your first invoice. They need it on file to report what they paid you. Ask your accountant about your own tax situation.

What should I do if an AV company pays late?

Follow up on the due date with the invoice attached, again a week later with the booker copied, and call if it's still unpaid after two or three weeks. Most late payments are stuck, not disputed.
