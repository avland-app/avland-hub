Quoting gear you don't own: sub-rentals done right

November 16, 2026 · 4 min read

A sub-rental is gear you rent from another company so you can supply it to your own client. Quote it like gear you own, add a markup that covers your risk and handling, and keep the vendor's cost on your internal sheet, not on the client's quote. Most sub-rental problems come from skipping one of those three steps.

Here is when to sub-rent, how to price it, what to confirm with the vendor, and how to keep the line clean on the quote.

When sub-renting makes sense

Sub-renting beats buying when the demand is rare. A second LED processor for one big show, extra wireless mics for a conference week, or a lens you need once a year all fit. It also helps when your own stock is already out on another job, or when a partner or sister company has spare gear sitting idle.

It is a bad deal when you do it every week for the same item. At that point, run the numbers on buying it. Our guide to setting day rates for AV rental gear shows how to work out what an owned item needs to earn.

How to price a sub-rental

You take on work and risk the client never sees: you find the item, confirm it, arrange pickup or delivery, test it, and answer for it if it fails on show day. The price has to cover that. There are three common ways to set it.

MethodHow it worksWatch out for
Cost plus a percentageVendor cost times a markup, such as cost + 30%A small percentage on cheap items does not cover your time
Your own day rateCharge your standard rate for that item as if you owned itNeeds a rate for every item you might sub-rent
Cost plus a flat feeVendor cost plus a set handling fee per item or per orderLarge orders can look cheap, small ones expensive

Cost-plus is the most common and the easiest to explain. The University of Illinois Chicago, for example, publishes that additional equipment can be rented at cost + 30%, with a per-item charge for same-day requests. That is one institution's schedule, not an industry rule, so set your own percentage based on how much work and risk the item adds.

A worked example

These numbers are made up to show the math. You sub-rent a video processor for a three-day show. The vendor charges you $900 for the three days, and your markup is 35%.

LineAmount
Vendor cost, 3 days$900
Markup, 35%$315
Price to client$1,215
Your gross profit$315

Now take out the real costs: a driver for pickup and return, an hour of prep and testing, and a damage deductible if the vendor charges one. If those add up to $200, you made $115 on the job. That is why many shops add a flat handling fee on top of the percentage, or never go below a minimum markup.

What to confirm with the vendor before you quote

  • Availability for the exact dates, including load-in and strike days.
  • How they count a day. If they charge 24 hours and you charge calendar days, you can end up paying for days you did not bill.
  • Delivery or pickup, who does it, and what it costs.
  • Damage and loss terms, including any deductible and who is responsible on site.
  • Substitutions. If they send a different model, you need to know before the client does.
  • A written confirmation of items, dates and price. A text thread is fine; a verbal yes is not.

How to show it on the quote

The client is buying a working system from you, so quote the item at your price like any other line. You do not need to show the vendor's cost. Do keep these two things:

  • A note on your internal sheet that the item is sub-rented, with the vendor, their cost and the confirmation. You will need it for the pull list, the pickup and the return.
  • Your usual quantity, days, rate and total on the client's line, so it reads like the rest of the gear.

If a sub-rented item changes, update the line and send a new revision instead of editing the total by hand. For how equipment lines sit on a full quote, see our guide to writing an AV quote.

Sharing gear with a sister company or partner

If you share gear with a sister company or another AV shop, treat it the same way: agree on a price list in advance, confirm dates, and record it as a sub-rental. Informal deals are where the double-booking and who-pays-for-damage arguments start.

Questions

What is a sub-rental in AV?

A sub-rental is equipment you rent from another company and supply to your own client, usually because you do not own it or your stock is already booked.

How much should I mark up sub-rented gear?

There is no standard figure. Many shops use cost plus a percentage and add a handling fee on small items. Set yours so it covers pickup, prep, testing and the risk of damage.

Should the client see the vendor's price?

No. The client buys the item from you at your price. Keep the vendor's cost on your internal sheet.

Who is responsible if sub-rented gear fails on site?

You are, from the client's point of view. Confirm the vendor's damage, loss and replacement terms in writing before the show.
