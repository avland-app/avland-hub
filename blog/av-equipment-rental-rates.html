Setting day rates for AV rental gear

November 2, 2026 · 6 min read

The right day rate for a piece of AV gear is the one that pays the gear back well before it wears out, at the number of days a year it actually works, and still sits where clients will book it. Start from what the item cost, decide how fast it has to pay for itself, assume realistic utilization, then check the result against your market.

This is the method, step by step, with a worked example you can copy into your own numbers.

Start with payback, not a percentage of price

You'll hear rules like "charge 1% of the purchase price per day." They're a fine sanity check, but they ignore two things that matter more: how many days the item really rents, and how long it stays useful. A rate built on a rule of thumb can look right and still lose money on a quiet year.

Work backwards instead. Pick the payback you need, then see what rate and utilization get you there.

  1. Total cost of the item. Purchase price plus cases, cables, adapters, and anything it can't run without.
  2. Target return. One practitioner essay in ESTA's Protocol magazine sets the minimum bar at three times the purchase price earned back over the life of the gear. Choose your own bar, but pick one on purpose.
  3. Useful life in years. How long before it's obsolete, worn out, or no longer what clients ask for.
  4. Utilization. The share of available days the item is actually out on a rental.

Be honest about utilization

Utilization is where rate sheets go wrong. The item that is booked solid in December is not the average item. The same ESTA piece makes the point with camera fleets: your busiest unit might be out 75% of the time, but across all of them the average is much lower, and buying extra units for one peak job drags the average down further.

It also warns that overestimating either the life of an item or its average utilization is "a sure way to miss long-term profitability." Read the full piece in the ESTA Protocol archive. It's one author's experience in video rental, not a standard, but the logic carries to audio, lighting and LED.

If you don't have your own history yet, use a number you'd be comfortable defending, not the best month you've had.

A worked example

These numbers are made up to show the math. Use your own.

StepExample
Total cost (item, case, cables)$8,000
Target: earn back 3x over the item's life$24,000
Useful life4 years
Needed per year$6,000
Realistic rental days per year (utilization)40
Day rate needed$150

Now stress it. If the item only works 25 days a year, the same target needs $240 a day. That gap is the risk you're taking on every time you buy, and it's why a high-demand item can carry a lower rate than a niche one.

Check the rate against the market

The math gives you a floor. The market gives you a ceiling. Look at what comparable rental houses in your area charge for the same item, and what the client's alternative is: renting from someone else or using the venue's house system. If your floor is above the market, the item is either a poor purchase or needs a longer rental period to pay off. If the market is well above your floor, you have room, but don't price so high that the gear sits.

Gear with very different demand shouldn't share one formula either. The ESTA piece notes that items in the same inventory can have vastly different payback expectations and recommends evaluating them separately. A commodity wireless mic and a specialty video processor are not the same business.

Build a rate card, then decide who gets discounts

Once each item has a day rate, put them on one rate card and keep it consistent. A rate card is your list price. Discounts for longer rentals, repeat clients or big packages come off it, and they should be your decision, not something improvised on a quote. Write down your standard rules so every quote follows them.

For multi-day jobs, decide how day two and three are charged before the request comes in. Our guide to writing an AV quote shows where rental days and rates sit on the quote itself.

Review rates once or twice a year

  • Actual days out. Compare last year's real utilization with the number you assumed.
  • Repairs and replacements. Gear that needs frequent repair shortens its useful life.
  • Sub-rentals. If you keep sub-renting the same item at a high share of its purchase price, buying it may beat renting it again. The ESTA essay lists that as a reason to buy.
  • Items that never go out. Raise the price, bundle it into a kit, or sell it.

Questions

How do I set a day rate for AV equipment I own?

Add up the full cost of the item and its accessories, choose a target return and useful life, estimate how many days a year it will realistically rent, and divide. Then compare the result with what other rental houses in your market charge.

Is there a standard percentage of purchase price for a day rate?

No. Percentage rules of thumb exist, but they ignore utilization and lifespan. Use them only as a quick sanity check on the number you calculate.

How often should I update my rental rates?

Review them once or twice a year against your actual days out, repair costs and what the market is charging, and update the items that have drifted.
